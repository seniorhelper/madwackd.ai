How to make an AI music video: how we made ours.

By Zach Wennstedt, founder of Madwackd Studio · Updated November 6, 2026

Short answer: you can make a music video with AI, but a good one isn't one prompt. Our two music videos, Tell Me Why (4:19) and What Love Really Is (5:01), each took an original song, a locked character design, dozens of AI shots, a frame-by-frame review, retakes, and many rounds of edits by a human producer.

Most guides for "how to make an AI music video for my song" say the same thing: upload your song, pick a style, press go. That gets you a video. It doesn't get you one people watch to the end. Here's what it actually took to make the two music videos in our Video Vault, written and produced by Zach Wennstedt, the founder of Madwackd Studio. We'll share the process and the lessons. The exact tools and prompts stay in the studio.

Step 1: Start with the song, then map every line

Both songs were written by Zach. Before making a single picture, we timed every sung line to a fraction of a second with speech-to-text. That timing sheet becomes the edit plan: which shot plays under which words, where the chorus lands, where a cut can hide.

Step 2: Lock the look before you make shots

The number one problem with AI video is drift: the same character looks like a different person in the next shot. So we make a reference first and hold every shot to it.

  • For Tell Me Why, the cowboy is Zach himself, made from his own photos with his consent. (We never make a real person's likeness without their OK. See our trust and safety rules.)
  • For What Love Really Is, we made a character sheet first: a blue neon crescent moon with a quiff and high-tops, and a pink neon star with a ponytail and boots. Every one of the 25 shots used that sheet as its guide.

Step 3: Stills first, then motion

Every shot starts as a still picture we approve, then becomes an 8-second moving clip. Stills are cheap and fast to fix. Clips cost more, so you only animate a frame you already like. Tell Me Why used 34 approved stills and 36 clips, cut together in 43 edits.

Step 4: Check every frame, and retake what's wrong

We sampled frames from every clip and wrote down what broke. Real examples from these two videos:

  • A neon star kept turning into a human girl. The cause was one word in the shot description. Removing it fixed the shots.
  • Characters doubled, merged into the signs behind them, or picked up readable text that wasn't supposed to be there.
  • In the human version of What Love Really Is, the face slowly drifted younger and narrower as he walked toward the camera.

For the neon video, 10 of 25 shots were retaken. Five still glitched after two tries, so instead of forcing them we used their approved still pictures with a slow camera push-in. Viewers can't tell, and the video stays clean.

Step 5: Know when to drop a feature

We tried AI lip-sync twice on Tell Me Why. It never looked right, so we cut every singing shot. The final video tells the story with looks, hands and horses instead. Sometimes the best fix is removing the thing that doesn't work.

Step 6: Edit like a producer, not a generator

The edit is where these videos came alive, and it's all human judgment:

  • Tiny timing fixes. One line in Tell Me Why came in about 3 seconds after the previous one and felt late. We made four test cuts (the original, and 0.5, 1 and 1.5 seconds sooner). Zach picked 0.5 seconds.
  • Repeating the best part. The walking chorus was the strongest moment of What Love Really Is, so we brought it back near the end. Making those repeats sound natural took four rounds: we measured where the song's energy and rhythm matched, cut only on the beat, and moved the whispered line onto the first melody.
  • Fixing what the viewer notices. A blemish on a close-up, two riders who touch hands for one second and let go (they now hold hands through every horse shot), a hand bent the wrong way. Small things, but they're what makes a video feel finished.
  • A whole new look when the first one isn't right. What Love Really Is started as a live-action-style video. Zach wanted something more original, so it was rebuilt as a neon cartoon, and that's the version on our site.

Step 7: Finish and credit it honestly

Both videos end with a title card: the song name, "Written and produced by Zach Wennstedt," and "A Madwackd Studio production." We don't say "made with one click," because they weren't.

Common questions

Can I make an AI music video for my song for free?

You can make short clips with free tools, and many apps offer a free try. A full-length video with consistent characters takes many clips plus retakes, so expect to pay for generation time or for someone's hours. Watch for watermarks and check whether the free plan allows commercial use.

How do I make an AI video of myself singing?

You need clear, well-lit photos of yourself and a tool that supports likeness with consent. Be ready for lip-sync to be the hardest part: it's where AI music videos most often look wrong. We dropped it entirely on Tell Me Why and told the story without singing shots.

How long does an AI music video take?

The renders are quick. The review and editing aren't. A one-prompt video can take minutes; a video you're proud of takes many rounds of checking, retakes and edits, often over days.

Do I have to say it was made with AI?

Platforms like TikTok and YouTube ask about AI-generated content when you upload. Answer honestly. Our AI disclosure page explains how we label videos.
